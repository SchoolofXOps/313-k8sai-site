Module 5 — AI-Aware Scheduling & Capacity

A training job has been Pending for forty minutes, and the cluster has idle accelerators. Nothing is broken. The scheduler is doing exactly what it was built to do, one Pod at a time, and that is the problem. This module is about the layer that sits above the scheduler on an AI platform: who decides whether a group of Pods may start at all, against whose quota, on which kind of capacity, inside which part of the network, and what happens when two teams want the same scarce thing at the same time.

  • Core path: ~4.0 h
  • Advanced brief: 1.0 h (labelled, optional)

You will read the same group of Pods placed seven-of-eight by the kube-scheduler and refused whole by a queue, starve one tenant and fix it with priority and preemption, count a synthetic device against quota in three postures, keep a group inside one topology block, and watch two quota systems disagree about one Job. The rest of this page will make more sense after.

Pod-at-a-time placement and the stranded group​

Imagine a restaurant that seats guests one at a time, as they walk in. A party of eight arrives. The host finds a free chair for the first guest, then the second, then the third, each at whichever table has a chair. Seven guests are seated. There is no chair left for the eighth. The seven sit at their tables, ordering nothing, because the party only eats together, and the restaurant charges them for the tables anyway. The eighth stands at the door forever.

That is how Kubernetes places Pods by default. The kube-scheduler takes one Pod from its queue, finds the best node for that Pod alone, binds it, and moves on to the next Pod. It has no idea that eight Pods belong together. For a web service that is fine: seven replicas out of eight still serve traffic. For a distributed training job it is a disaster. Training Pods usually wait for every member before any of them does useful work, so seven placed Pods hold seven accelerators and do nothing, and the eighth never arrives. People call this the 7-of-8 trap.

The lab shows it with real numbers. The simulated fleet has nine nodes at FLEET=collision, and each one has 7.9 cpu free, read from the nodes on the course's own run (8 cpu allocatable, less 100m that the network agent on every node asks for). A tenant called research already runs two Pods of 4 cpu, on two different nodes, because two of them would need 8 cpu and a node has only 7.9. Then a group of eight Pods, each asking for 4 cpu, is submitted with no queue in front of it. Work it out on paper first:

  • a node that already holds one 4-cpu Pod has 3.9 cpu free, and 3.9 is less than 4, so it can take no more;
  • two nodes are already full from research;
  • that leaves 9 − 2 = 7 nodes, and each of them can take exactly one of the eight.

So the scheduler places seven Pods and leaves one Pending, and the lab reads exactly that: seven Pods with .spec.nodeName set, one without, and that one's PodScheduled condition naming Insufficient cpu.

Be honest about the scope of this example. The fleet's Pods ask for cpu, not accelerators. The arithmetic is the same arithmetic an accelerator fleet runs on, but nothing in this lab holds a real device on the simulated nodes, and nothing here measures a training job.

There are more problems behind this one. A scheduler that packs one Pod at a time also fragments the fleet: it leaves a little capacity free on many nodes, and no single node has room for a large Pod. And it has no notion of a job, a queue, a quota a team can borrow, or a fair share between teams. Those are the things the rest of this module adds.

Admission, placement, creation and device allocation​

Back to the restaurant. A good restaurant splits the work. The host at the door decides whether your party is seated now or waits, and how many tables your party is allowed. The floor manager decides which tables. The kitchen staff bring the plates and replace one that drops. And the sommelier decides which bottle from the cellar goes to your table. Four people, four questions, and nobody does someone else's job.

An AI platform on Kubernetes splits the work the same way, and most confusion in this field comes from mixing the four up:

  • Whether a group runs now, against which quota and which kind of capacity, is an admission decision.
  • Where each Pod goes is a placement decision, and the kube-scheduler makes it.
  • How Pods are created, restarted and finished is the workload controller's job: the Job, StatefulSet, JobSet or training-job controller.
  • Which device a Pod gets, and making that device ready on the node, is Dynamic Resource Allocation (DRA) and the device's driver.

Gang behaviour, the "all of them or none of them" property, is not a feature of one component. It comes from the workload type plus the mechanisms you configure around it. Kubernetes now has two paths for the admission half. Here they are side by side.

Gang scheduling in the core scheduling API​

Kubernetes is now growing its own answer to the 7-of-8 trap. In Kubernetes 1.37 the core Workload and PodGroup objects are served at scheduling.k8s.io/v1beta1. A PodGroup says "these Pods belong together, and at least this many of them must be placed at once". The scheduler then treats the group as one unit: it binds the members together or holds all of them back. The same objects carry workload-aware preemption, where the scheduler evicts a whole group rather than single Pods.

All of it sits behind one feature gate, GenericWorkload. Two earlier gates were removed in 1.37 and folded into this one, so you will see the old names only in material written for 1.36 or before.

GenericWorkload and DRAWorkloadResourceClaims are beta and default-off in Kubernetes 1.37 clusters; this course's grid-core profile turns both on.

Default-off means off in almost every cluster you will meet at work. The grid-core profile turns both on so you can read the objects, and the deep dive places a core gang on the simulated fleet. DRAWorkloadResourceClaims lets one ResourceClaim belong to a whole PodGroup, so a group that shares one device asks for it once.

Turning the gate on has a side effect this course's own profile runs into. With GenericWorkload on, there are two kinds named Workload in the cluster: the core Workload in scheduling.k8s.io and the Kueue Workload in kueue.x-k8s.io. A plain read by the short plural name resolves to only one of them, so it can print nothing while the other kind holds every object you are looking for. That is why every command in this module names the full resource, workloads.kueue.x-k8s.io or workloads.scheduling.k8s.io, and why the lab's first part makes you see the empty read for yourself.

Two scheduler extension points carry the group logic: PlacementFeasible, which runs after each member is tried and decides whether the group can still be placed whole, and PodGroupPostFilter, which runs when the group cannot be scheduled.

Several parts of this area are taught here and not built in the lab. Each one has a reason:

  • The silent-pending failure with DRAWorkloadResourceClaims off. With that gate off, the ResourceClaim controller deliberately does not create a per-Pod claim for a PodGroup member, so the Pod waits with no claim and no error. The grid-core profile has the gate on, and the course does not create a second, gate-off cluster for one sentence.
  • The 1.36 to 1.37 upgrade trap. The core Workload and PodGroup moved from v1alpha2 in 1.36 to v1beta1 in 1.37, and every v1alpha2 core Workload and PodGroup object must be deleted from the API server before the upgrade. No 1.36 cluster exists in this course to show it on.
  • CompositePodGroup, groups of groups, served at scheduling.k8s.io/v1alpha3. It is alpha, and this course does not build on alpha APIs.
  • The workloadbuilder library and the KEP-6089 building blocks, a shared way for JobSet, training jobs and Ray jobs to describe their groups to the scheduler. Also alpha.
  • Kueue's emerging DRA checks. KueueDRADeviceFeasibility, which checks before admission that a device the group asks for can actually be placed, and KueueDRAIntegrationPrioritizedList, which accounts device requests with alternatives. Both are alpha and off by default at Kueue v0.20.6. They are taught as where Kueue is going.
  • MultiKueue, which dispatches work from one manager cluster to several worker clusters. It needs at least two clusters, so it lives in the briefs at the end of this page.
  • Elastic workloads and dynamic reclaim, where a running group grows, shrinks, or hands back quota it no longer uses. They change admission over a group's lifetime, and the lab's groups have fixed sizes.

The honest framing is this: the core path is real, it is in your API server today if someone turns the gate on, and it is not finished. Compare it with Kueue's admission as you go. The deep dive does exactly that on one cluster: the same ten-Pod group placed whole or not at all by the kube-scheduler on the core path, and admitted by Kueue on quota and then only partly placed by the fleet.

Quota, queues and admission​

Think of a shared office kitchen with a fridge. Each team has its own shelf. A team may borrow an empty shelf from another team, but when the owner comes back with groceries, the borrower has to clear it. And the office manager can say "the sales team may never lend more than one of its shelves", so sales always has room. That is quota with borrowing, lending and reclaim, and it is what Kueue adds to Kubernetes.

Kueue is a queueing and quota controller. It does not replace the scheduler. Its objects, all at kueue.x-k8s.io/v1beta2:

  • LocalQueue — a team's entry point, in the team's namespace. A workload joins it by carrying the label kueue.x-k8s.io/queue-name.
  • ClusterQueue — the quota, cluster-scoped: how much cpu, memory and device capacity of each kind the queues pointing at it may use.
  • ResourceFlavor — a kind of capacity, described by node labels and tolerations. Two flavours with the same quota resource are two different pools: in the lab, sim-fleet is the simulated nodes and real-worker is the one real worker.
  • Cohort — a set of ClusterQueues that may borrow each other's unused quota. A ClusterQueue joins one by naming it in cohortName.
  • Kueue Workload — the object Kueue creates for each group it manages. Its conditions record the decision: QuotaReserved, Admitted, Evicted, Preempted, each with a reason you can read.
  • WorkloadPriorityClass — a priority for a group, read only by Kueue.

The admission mechanism itself is simple. A Job is created with suspend: true, or a StatefulSet's Pods are created with a scheduling gate, so nothing can run. Kueue looks at the group's total request, finds a flavour in the ClusterQueue with room, writes the decision onto the Kueue Workload, and only then unsuspends the Job or lifts the gate. That is why a queued group is all or nothing: the quota for every member is reserved in one step, or none is.

Around that core sit the controls a platform team actually tunes:

  • Priority. A higher-priority group is considered first. On its own, that changes only the order in the queue.
  • Preemption. A ClusterQueue's preemption policy says whether a waiting group may evict others: inside its own queue (withinClusterQueue), or across the cohort to take back quota someone else borrowed (reclaimWithinCohort). Without a policy, a higher priority takes nothing back. The lab shows exactly that, and then fixes it.
  • Borrowing and lending limits. borrowingLimit caps how much a queue may take from the cohort; lendingLimit caps how much of its own quota it will ever lend.
  • Fair sharing, which weighs how far each queue is above its share when Kueue chooses whom to admit or preempt.

The course pins Kueue v0.20.6, a patch release on the v0.20 line whose fixes touch borrowing, preemption, StatefulSet quota and topology admission, all paths this module teaches.

Kueue is not only for batch jobs. Its integrations include Deployments, StatefulSets and LeaderWorkerSets, so an inference service's capacity can be put under the same quota as training. That is the link to Module 8. Kueue also admits a JobSet or a LeaderWorkerSet group whole, reserving quota for all of its Pods at once, without a second scheduler. Neither is installed in this module; Module 7 runs JobSet.

Device accounting against quota​

A device is only governed by a quota if Kueue can see it and count it. Kueue counts a claimed device through a device-class mapping in its own configuration: "a claim for DeviceClass sim-accelerator.example.com counts as one unit of the quota resource example.com/sim-accelerator". The lab turns that mapping, and Kueue's DRA integration, off and on, and reads the result on the Kueue Workload each time. At Kueue v0.20.6, on this course's cluster, the course's run showed three postures:

  • The integration switched off (four DRA gates set to false together): the device workload is admitted and charged nothing. Its claim is still allocated a synthetic device by the scheduler, but the device never appears in Kueue's accounting. This is the only posture that charges a device workload nothing.
  • The mapping removed, default gates: the workload is refused, with QuotaReserved=False and reason DRAResourcesUnresolved, because the DeviceClass "is not mapped".
  • The mapping present, default gates: the device is counted, and against a device quota of 0 the count refuses it, with reason ExceedsMaxQuota.

There is a piece of folklore here worth correcting: that a device with no mapping is quietly free. At this release it is not. It is refused, loudly, with a reason that names the unmapped class.

A Pod that names a claim created ahead of time, through resourceClaimName instead of a template, is also refused, with reason Misconfigured. Kueue v0.20.6 counts devices only through ResourceClaimTemplates. Treat that as a documented limitation of this release, not as a hole: the deep dive shows the refusal.

Who decides what on the simulated fleet​

The lab's fleet is attached to a real control plane, and three different things act on every Pod there, in this order. Kueue picks the quota and, for a topology flavour, the domain, and writes them into the Pod. The real kube-scheduler on grid-core picks the node and binds the Pod. Then kwok, the tool that runs the simulated nodes, fakes the kubelet's work, after a binding it had no part in. The fleet is attached to grid-core rather than run under a separate fake control plane because kwok's own cluster images stop at Kubernetes 1.36, and this course pins 1.37. A fake scheduler deciding about fake nodes would prove nothing; here, every binding is the real scheduler's.

Topology-constrained admission​

Eight accelerators on one machine are not the same as one accelerator on each of eight machines. Groups that talk to each other constantly want to sit close together: the same node, the same rack, the same block of racks under one switch. Data centres describe that closeness with node labels, from wide to narrow: zone, block, rack, host.

Kueue's Topology-Aware Scheduling turns those labels into an admission rule. A Topology object lists the label keys from the widest level to the narrowest, a ResourceFlavor points at it with topologyName, and a group asks for its domain with a Pod-template annotation:

  • kueue.x-k8s.io/podset-required-topology — the whole group must fit inside one domain at that level, or it is not admitted;
  • kueue.x-k8s.io/podset-preferred-topology — fit in one domain if possible, and spread across the fewest domains if not.

Topology-Aware Scheduling is beta and on by default since Kueue v0.14.

The lab's fleet has three blocks of three racks, one node per rack. Each group asks for 12Gi per Pod, so a node takes two and a block takes six. Three groups of four that each require one block land in three different blocks. A fourth identical group is not admitted, because no block still has room for four. Its Kueue Workload waits with reason WaitingForQuota, the same reason a group short of quota gets, and only the message names the topology: it fits two of the four Pods in any one block. So read a waiting group's message, not only its reason. And the same group with a preferred block is admitted across two blocks.

Be precise about what that shows. It shows topology-constrained admission and placement: which domain a group was allowed into, read from each Pod's .spec.nodeName and that node's block label. It says nothing about bandwidth, NVLink versus PCIe, RDMA or how fast a collective operation runs. Those are hardware properties, and Module 6 covers them on rented and recorded hardware.

Tenants, two quota systems and showback​

A cluster shared by teams needs walls. Namespaces separate the teams' objects. RBAC per tenant, a Role and a RoleBinding in each tenant's namespace, decides who may create what there, and who may edit the queues. Priority classes decide whose work goes first. And quota decides how much each team may hold.

Here is the trap: Kubernetes has two quota systems, and they do not talk to each other. A namespace ResourceQuota is checked by the API server every time a Pod is created. Kueue's quota is checked once, when a group is admitted. Picture a building where the receptionist hands your guest a visitor badge, and then the turnstile refuses the badge because the floor is full. Both systems are right by their own rules, and the guest is stuck in the lobby.

The lab reproduces that classic incident on cpu alone. The product namespace gets a ResourceQuota of 8 cpu of requests, which its serving group already uses up, while product-cq still has quota to spare. A small suspended Job arrives. Kueue admits it, because the ClusterQueue has room. The Job controller then tries to create the Pod, and the API server refuses it against the namespace quota. The result is an admitted Kueue Workload, a Job with no Pod, and FailedCreate events naming exceeded quota: product-quota. Nothing is Pending, because no Pod exists to be pending, which is why this incident is so confusing when you meet it for real. The fix is a design decision, not a command: pick one system to govern a resource for a namespace, and make the other one generous enough that it never decides.

Then showback. Once quota is in Kueue, the ClusterQueue's own status already says how much each team holds, how much it borrows and how much it lends: .status.flavorsUsage, per flavour and per resource. Multiply the device and accelerator-hour usage by a price and you have a per-team report. That brings back Module 2's four levers, now with numbers you can read from the cluster: how much each team is guaranteed, how much it may borrow, how fast idle capacity is handed back, and who pays for what was held. Notebook servers and other idle workloads are the classic leak, which is why platforms add time limits and reaping.

How big can the simulated fleet get on a laptop? The queueing layer reached 1000 simulated nodes and 2020 Pods at 3820 MiB (conditions: the 8 GB one-worker grid-core profile at SIZE=8, Rancher Desktop on an Apple M1 Pro, kind v0.33.0 with Kubernetes 1.37, kwok v0.8.0 attached to the real control plane, the summed node container peak read once the fleet settled, on the course's own run). That is a statement about that one profile and nothing more. FLEET=scale brings that size up if you want to try it as an optional extension; the lab itself runs nine nodes.

The operational cost of a second scheduler​

Several rows above are a second scheduler, a binary that places Pods instead of, or beside, the kube-scheduler. The cost of that is easy to underestimate, so here it is plainly. A second scheduler is a second program that must agree with the kube-scheduler about every node's free capacity, at every moment, while both are binding Pods; when they disagree, both place a Pod on the same last free slot and one of them fails at the kubelet. It is a second set of upgrades, tied to its own Kubernetes version support. And it is two placement decisions to debug: a Pending Pod might be waiting for either scheduler, and the events you need are split between them. This course does not install one. Kueue plus the kube-scheduler covers queueing and gang admission without it.

The HAMi row is a different kind of tool. It shares one accelerator between several Pods with a memory quota per Pod, across several vendors' devices, through a scheduler extender and a device plugin, and it sits beside Kueue rather than replacing it: Kueue's own documentation runs HAMi's resources under Kueue's quota. HAMi-core's memory cap is enforced only when CUDA runs, and nothing in this course runs CUDA, so this course makes no claim about what HAMi isolates. The KAI row is worth a second look for the same reason: its sharing is not memory-enforced by default, and HAMi-core is one way to enforce it.

Fleet and multi-cluster. Before you split a platform across clusters, be sure one cluster is really full. A single cluster with good queues is the cheapest thing to operate. A cell-based design, several identical clusters each serving a slice of the tenants, limits how far one failure spreads. A true multi-cluster design dispatches one queue's work to whichever cluster has room, which is what MultiKueue does, and cloud bursting is the same idea pointed at rented capacity. Each step adds a control plane to run and a new way for work to go missing, so take it when the numbers force you to, not before.

Slurm and Kubernetes. Sites with years of Slurm job scripts rarely convert in one step. The choice is to migrate the jobs, bridge the two so Slurm keeps placing work for the users who want it, as slurm-bridge does, or run both side by side. The Slurm brief above compares the bridges.

Where ownership sits​

Every mechanism in this module has exactly one owner on the Grid's path. When something goes wrong, the first question is which one.

MechanismOwner
decides whether a group is admitted now, against which quota and flavourKueue
places each admitted Pod and binds it to a nodekube-scheduler
creates, restarts and completes a group's Podsworkload controller
allocates the device a claim namesDRA

The workload controller row means the StatefulSet or Job controller in this module's lab, and the JobSet or training-job controller in Module 7. The DRA row is the scheduler's DRA plugin, which writes the allocation onto the claim.
